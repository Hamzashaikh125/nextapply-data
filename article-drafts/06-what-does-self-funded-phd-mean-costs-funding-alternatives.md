**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

A self-funded PhD is one where you — not a university, funder or employer — pay tuition fees and living costs for three to four years. In the UK that realistically means £60,000–£100,000+ in total; in Germany or the Nordics, where PhDs are salaried jobs, self-funding is usually unnecessary.

---

## What "self-funded" actually means

When a PhD offer or advert says "self-funded students welcome", it translates to: we will supervise you, but no money is attached. You cover two things:

- **Tuition fees**, paid annually to the university — for international students in the UK commonly in the £20,000–£35,000 per year range (varies widely by university and subject; verify current fee tables)
- **Living costs**, for the full duration — in the UK roughly £12,000–£15,000+ per year depending on the city, and more in London

There is no stipend, no salary, and usually no expectation that one will appear later (though some self-funded students do win funding mid-degree).

## Realistic totals by country

| Country | Tuition (international, approx.) | Typical duration | What you actually pay |
|---|---|---|---|
| UK | ~£20,000–£35,000/year | 3–4 years | Often £80,000–£120,000 total including living costs |
| Germany | Public universities: no tuition (semester fee only) | 3–4 years | Living costs only (~€950–€1,200/month); PhDs often paid as jobs instead |
| Netherlands | Universities charge fees, but PhD posts are salaried employment | 4 years | Usually €0 — you are an employee on ~€2,900–€3,700/month gross |
| Nordics (DK, NO, SE, FI) | €0 — doctoral candidates are employees | 3–4 years | €0 — salaried positions |
| USA | Funded by stipends/assistantships at research universities | 5–6 years | Admissions and funding are usually bundled; "unfunded offers" are a red flag |

## Why self-funding a UK PhD is risky

Honest assessment matters here, so plainly:

- **The full cost is enormous.** Six figures for a UK PhD is more than many people's graduate salary for several years.
- **Completion risk rises with money pressure.** Students who must work alongside a self-funded PhD take longer and complete at lower rates.
- **"Just find funding later" rarely works.** Mid-degree scholarships are rare and competitive; do not enrol on the assumption one will appear.
- **Visa financial requirements assume funding.** For a UK student visa you must show maintenance funds — a self-funded route means proving that money is genuinely yours.

None of this means self-funding is always wrong. It can make sense for a short, part-time professional doctorate, or when an employer shares the cost. It is a bad default for a recent graduate.

## Alternatives that cost far less

Before accepting a self-funded offer, work through these routes:

1. **Salaried PhD positions in Europe.** Germany (TV-L E13 positions), the Netherlands, Norway, Denmark and Switzerland treat doctoral candidates as employees with salaries, benefits and no tuition. Competition exists, but so do far more positions than named scholarships. Our guide on [how to read a PhD job advertisement](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-read-phd-job-advertisement-before-applying) shows how to check whether a post is genuinely funded and open to international candidates.
2. **Fully funded scholarships.** Gates Cambridge (~£18,744 stipend plus fees — verify current figures), Rhodes (£21,060 — verify), Commonwealth, Chevening (Master's) and national schemes like DAAD (€1,400/month for doctoral students — verify on daad.de). Browse current [fully funded scholarships on NextApply](https://nextapply.co.uk/fully-funded-scholarships).
3. **UKRI-funded projects.** UK doctoral training programmes and project studentships pay a tax-free stipend (recently around £19,000–£20,000 per year — verify the current rate) plus fees. You apply for a project, not for "funding" in the abstract.
4. **Structured programmes abroad.** If cost is the driver, [studying in Germany for free](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/study-in-germany-for-free-complete-guide-2027) or applying to Nordic PhD posts removes the fee problem entirely rather than working around it.
5. **Employer or government sponsorship.** Some employers fund doctorates tied to your role; some governments fund students who return to work at home.

## If you do go self-funded: how to reduce the damage

| Strategy | Effect |
|---|---|
| Choose a low-fee country (Germany, Nordics) | Eliminates the largest cost block entirely |
| Part-time PhD alongside employment | Slower, but removes the living-cost cliff |
| Apply to every small bursary and fee waiver | Rarely covers everything; shaves thousands |
| Negotiate fees or a studentship at year 2–3 | Occasionally possible; never plan around it |
| Secure funding *before* enrolling | The only strategy that genuinely removes the risk |

The last row is the important one. The standard advice — and it is good advice — is: **do not start a self-funded PhD in the hope of funding later. Get the funding first, then enrol.**

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a self-funded PhD worth less than a funded one?**
The degree itself is identical. What differs is your experience: funded students focus on research, self-funded students often juggle work, which can affect output and completion time. Employers rarely ask how you funded it.

**Can I get a scholarship after starting a self-funded PhD?**
Occasionally — some university bursaries, country-specific schemes and mid-degree awards exist — but they are scarce and competitive. Never enrol on this hope.

**Do UK universities offer funding to international PhD students?**
Yes, through UKRI studentships, university scholarships and project funding, but international-eligible places are limited and very competitive. Apply early in the cycle and treat self-funding as the fallback, not the plan.

**Which countries pay PhD students a salary?**
Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, Denmark, Sweden, Finland and Switzerland all offer salaried doctoral positions in most fields. Check whether the position requires a Master's first — many do.
