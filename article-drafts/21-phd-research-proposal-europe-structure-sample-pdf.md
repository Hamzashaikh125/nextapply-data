**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

A PhD research proposal for a European university is typically 1,500–3,000 words with seven parts: title, problem statement and context, research questions and objectives, literature positioning, methodology, timeline, and expected contribution. There is no universal downloadable PDF template — each university differs — so build the structure below into your own document, and save this page as a reference PDF if useful.

## First: do you actually need a proposal?

Before writing anything, confirm which application type you are facing. The requirements differ fundamentally:

| Application type | Proposal needed? | What you write instead |
|---|---|---|
| Self-proposed PhD (UK, much of Europe) | Yes — the core document | — |
| Advertised funded position (Netherlands, Nordics, Germany) | Usually no | Cover letter + fit statement |
| Scholarship schemes (DAAD, Commonwealth, Marshall) | Often yes | Follow the scheme's exact template |
| Structured programmes / doctoral schools | Sometimes a short 2–3 page version | Check the call text |

Advertised positions dominate in the Netherlands, Germany and Scandinavia — the project is pre-designed, and your energy goes into a tailored cover letter (see our [PhD cover letter sample](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-write-cover-letter-phd-application-sample)). Self-proposed applications in the UK and for many scholarships are where full proposals matter. Our guide to [reading a PhD job advert](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-read-phd-job-advertisement-before-applying) helps you tell them apart quickly.

## The seven-part structure (with word budget for a 2,000-word proposal)

### 1. Title (one line)
Descriptive, not clever: *topic + method + setting*. "Machine learning approaches to flood forecasting in ungauged African basins" says everything; "Waves of change" says nothing.

### 2. Problem statement and context (~300 words)
What real or scientific problem does the research address? Open with the problem, not with "Since the dawn of time". Give the reader: the phenomenon, why it matters, and the gap your work targets. Two or three well-chosen citations establish that the field exists and that you read it.

### 3. Research questions and objectives (~150 words)
Two to four numbered questions — specific enough that an answer would be checkable. Bad: "How does climate change affect agriculture?" Good: "How have shifting rainfall patterns between 1990 and 2025 altered maize planting dates in central Malawi, and what do farmers identify as the primary adaptive constraints?"

### 4. Literature positioning (~400 words)
Not a literature review — a positioning exercise. Identify the 3–4 strands of work your project sits between, name the leading contributors, and state precisely what remains unresolved. The sentence committees look for is the one that says: "However, no study has yet addressed X using Y in context Z." That sentence *is* your justification.

### 5. Methodology (~500 words — the section that decides everything)
Specify:

- **Design:** quantitative, qualitative, mixed, experimental, modelling, archival
- **Data:** what you will collect or use, from where, sample sizes or spatial scope, and access feasibility
- **Methods of analysis:** named techniques and software — "thematic analysis (NVivo)", "structural equation modelling in R (lavaan)"
- **Limitations and alternatives:** one honest paragraph on what could go wrong and your plan B

Supervisors read section 5 twice as carefully as everything else. A brilliant question with a vague method reads as inexperience; a modest question with a rigorous, feasible method reads as fundable.

### 6. Timeline (~150 words + table)
A simple Gantt-style table across the standard European PhD length (3 years in the UK and Netherlands; 3–5 in Germany and the Nordics):

| Phase | Months | Activities |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1–6 | Literature synthesis, research design, ethics application |
| 2 | 7–18 | Fieldwork / data collection (two field visits planned) |
| 3 | 19–30 | Analysis, first two papers, conference presentation |
| 4 | 31–36 | Third paper, thesis writing, submission |

### 7. Expected contribution and references (~200 words + list)
State what the thesis adds — empirically, theoretically, methodologically — and one line on who benefits. Then a short reference list in a consistent style (whatever the department uses; APA or Harvard are safe defaults).

## Worked micro-example (problem → question → method)

To see the logic connect, here is one proposal's spine:

> **Problem:** Flood early-warning systems in West Africa rely on sparse gauge networks, and forecast models trained on data-rich basins perform poorly when transferred.
> **Question:** Can transfer-learning methods, fine-tuned with locally collected crowdsourced rainfall data, improve 48-hour flood forecasts in the Ogun basin relative to regional models?
> **Method:** Comparative modelling study. Dataset A: ERA5 reanalysis and GPM satellite precipitation (2010–2025). Dataset B: 12 months of crowdsourced gauge readings from 40 community stations (partnership with the state emergency agency already established). Models: LSTM baseline versus fine-tuned transfer-learning architecture, evaluated by Kling-Gupta efficiency and false-alarm rate. Limitation: crowdsourced data quality is variable; mitigation: automated outlier screening and a manual validation subset.
> **Contribution:** methodological (a validated transfer pipeline for ungauged basins) and practical (an operational tool for the emergency agency).

Every proposal should compress to something this concrete. If yours cannot, the thinking is not finished.

## Writing a proposal without a confirmed supervisor

Common for self-proposed applications. The sequence that works:

1. Define your topic narrowly enough that one person's research group would plausibly host it.
2. Read that group's last three years of papers; position your questions in their conversation.
3. Draft the proposal, then email the potential supervisor — our [supervisor email templates](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-email-phd-supervisor-uk-7-templates) show how to attach a proposal and ask for feedback without presuming commitment.
4. Revise with their input. A proposal a supervisor has commented on is effectively a pre-agreed application.

## What committees are actually checking

- **Feasibility.** Can this be done in 3–4 years by one person with realistic resources? The single biggest rejection reason.
- **Fit.** Does the department have the expertise and data environment to host it?
- **Originality.** Genuinely new, not necessarily world-changing — an unanswered question suffices.
- **Writing quality.** Clear, structured, correctly referenced prose predicts a PhD that finishes.

Weaknesses in your profile (grades, no publications, a gap) do not need to appear in the proposal itself — the proposal is judged as a document. Handle profile concerns in your statement or cover letter using our [academic gap guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-explain-academic-gap-university-application), and remember that most European PhDs do not require prior papers — see [starting a PhD without publications](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-in-europe-without-publications).

## Practical notes on format and the "PDF template" question

There is no official Europe-wide proposal template, and we won't pretend otherwise: every university and scholarship scheme specifies its own format, and where one exists it overrides everything here. What transfers between institutions is the *structure above*. Practical tips:

- Build the seven sections in a clean document with the heading hierarchy shown here; it will pass most institutional checks after light edits.
- Save this page as a PDF from your browser (Print → Save as PDF) so you have the outline to hand while writing.
- Verify your target university's stated limit (some UK funders cap at 1,000 words; DAAD and similar schemes have their own forms) before finalising length.
- Once your proposal is ready, the next stage is defending it in person — our [PhD interview questions guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-interview-questions-answers-research-interview) covers the questions committees ask about proposals.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a PhD research proposal be in Europe?**
Between 1,500 and 3,000 words for most self-proposed applications. Follow the specific university's or funder's limit where one is given — a 3,000-word proposal sent where 1,000 was requested is a compliance failure, however good the content.

**Can I write a proposal without knowing the exact methods I'll use?**
You must still propose concrete methods — "I will explore several approaches" fails. It is understood that methods evolve; committees want evidence you can design a rigorous plan now and name its risks.

**Do I need publications to submit a strong proposal?**
No. European committees judge the proposal on feasibility, originality and fit. Prior work helps but is not expected for most positions — see our guide on [PhDs without publications](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-in-europe-without-publications).

**Can I reuse one proposal for several universities?**
The core (problem, questions, method) can be reused; the literature positioning and "fit" framing must be adapted to each department's expertise, or supervisors will see a document written for someone else.
