**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

PhD interviews usually last 30–60 minutes and test three things: whether you understand your proposed research, whether you can think on your feet when challenged, and whether the supervisor can work with you. Prepare answers to around ten predictable questions, know your own project cold, and prepare two or three questions to ask them.

---

## How PhD interviews actually work

Formats vary more than applicants expect, so confirm details before the day:

| Format | Where common | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Panel with supervisor + 2 academics | UK, structured programmes | Project deep-dive + generic questions |
| One-to-one with supervisor | Advertised positions (NL, DE, Nordics often two rounds) | Conversational; your fit with the project |
| Presentation (10 min) + questions | Funded studentships, doctoral schools | Your proposal or Master's work, then grilling |
| Scholarship panel | DAAD, Commonwealth, university scholarships | Motivation, career plan, funding rationale — see our [DAAD guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/daad-scholarships-international-students-complete-guide) |

Whatever the format, the assessment is the same: *Can this person do a doctorate here, and will the working relationship survive four years?* Panels forgive nerves; they don't forgive not knowing your own proposal.

## The ten questions you should expect

### 1. "Tell us about yourself" / "Why do you want to do a PhD?"
Not a biography request. Give a 90-second narrative: degree → the specific problem or experience that pulled you into this topic → why a doctorate is the right tool for it → why now. Practise it aloud until it flows, but don't memorise it word-for-word or you'll sound robotic when nervous.

### 2. "Tell us about your research" / "Summarise your proposal"
Have a three-sentence version ready before they ask: *what problem, what method, what contribution*. The most common failure is rambling through chronology. State the gap, your approach, and the outcome you expect — then stop and let them probe.

### 3. "What is the main weakness of your project?"
This is a thinking test, not a trap. Name a real limitation — data access, method assumptions, timescale — and your mitigation. "The crowdsourced rainfall data will have quality issues; I've planned automated screening plus a manually validated subset." Never say "I can't think of any" — that answer ends more interviews than any weak project does.

### 4. "Why this university / this group / this supervisor?"
Specific and honest: their methods, a paper, a dataset or facility, the group's direction. "Your group's recent work on transfer learning for ungauged basins is the direct predecessor of my proposed method" — that sentence shows you did the reading, which doubles as evidence of research skill.

### 5. "What will you do if your main hypothesis/experiment fails?"
Panels want evidence of a plan B and of intellectual honesty. Sketch the fallback: alternative data, alternative method, or reframing the null result — "a rigorous demonstration that X doesn't work under these conditions is itself publishable."

### 6. "Describe a research challenge you've overcome"
Answer with a specific story from your Master's or project work: the obstacle, what you tried, what you learned. Structure it — situation, action, result. A story about a dataset that refused to cooperate says more than any adjective about your resilience.

### 7. "What are your strengths and weaknesses as a researcher?"
Strength: one technical, one personal, each with a one-line proof. Weakness: real and non-fatal, plus what you're doing about it — "I over-engineer early analysis; I now time-box exploratory work and agree milestones with collaborators." Avoid humble-bragging ("I'm too much of a perfectionist") — panels hear it hourly.

### 8. "How do you handle disagreement with a supervisor?"
The honest answer is the strong one: you seek disagreement early because it improves the work. Describe a real instance of pushing back on feedback, how you presented your case, and what happened. Panels are quietly screening for someone who will neither sulk nor cave — a four-year relationship is at stake.

### 9. "Where do you see yourself after the PhD?"
Plausible beats grand. Academia is fine; industry and policy are equally fine. Name a direction and connect it to the training the position provides. For scholarship interviews, a credible contribution plan (return home, sector, institution type) often matters more — see our [explaining gaps and unusual paths guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-explain-academic-gap-university-application) if your trajectory is non-linear.

### 10. "Do you have any questions for us?"
Never say no. Strong options:

- "What does a typical first year look like for a student in this group?"
- "How does the group support movement toward publication — is first-authorship expected in year one or two?"
- "What happens to the project if [key dataset/partnership] falls through?"
- For advertised positions: "What are the group's main funding streams over the next four years?"

Two or three good questions signal the seriousness of someone comparing offers, not begging for one.

## Preparing in the final week

1. **Re-read your own documents.** Your CV, proposal and cover letter are the interview script — expect questions drawn from every claim in them. If you wrote it, defend it.
2. **Re-read their two most recent papers.** Annotate one genuine question or connection about each.
3. **Rehearse out loud**, ideally on video. Reading answers in your head is not practice.
4. **Prepare the three-sentence versions** of: your project, your Master's thesis, your reason for this position.
5. **Check logistics** — time zone, platform, whether you must present slides, the panel members' names and roles. Getting a name or role wrong is avoidable damage.
6. **Plan your environment** for online interviews: quiet room, wired connection if possible, camera at eye level, a visible notebook for their names.

## Mistakes that sink otherwise strong candidates

- **Reciting the proposal verbatim** when asked about it — summarise and interpret instead.
- **Defensiveness under challenge.** When a panel member pokes a hole, they want to see your thinking, not a surrender or a fight. "That's a fair concern — my current thinking is X, though I'd want to test Y" is the ideal register.
- **Overclaiming expertise.** If asked about a method you don't know: "I haven't used it, but it connects to X I've done, and I'd want to learn it because of Y." Honest and constructive beats bluffing every time.
- **No questions at the end** — reads as either disinterest or panic.
- **Forgetting it's mutual.** You are also assessing the supervisor. Ask what happened to recent graduates from the group; the answer tells you a lot.

If the interview doesn't lead to an offer, it is rarely a verdict on your potential — projects are few and fits are specific. ## Frequently asked questions

**How long is a PhD interview?**
Typically 30–60 minutes. Scholarship panels and funded-studentship interviews can run longer, especially with a presentation. The invitation email usually states the format — if it doesn't, ask.

**What should I wear to a PhD interview?**
Whatever you'd wear teaching a class: smart-casual is safe everywhere in Europe. For online interviews, presentability from the waist up and a clean background matter more than formality.

**Can I get a PhD position without publications or research experience?**
Yes — especially for taught-Master's-to-PhD routes and many European positions. Interviews weight your proposal understanding and thinking over your publication list. See our guide to [PhDs without publications](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-in-europe-without-publications).

**Should I mention other offers or applications in the interview?**
If asked about timelines, be truthful — panels know candidates apply broadly, and honest constraints sometimes speed decisions. Don't manufacture leverage; it reads badly and is usually transparent.
