**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

A blocked account (Sperrkonto) is a special German account proving you can fund your studies: you deposit about €11,904 for one year (2026 visa rate — verify), and it releases a fixed monthly amount once you arrive. Most non-EU students need one — or an equivalent proof of funds — before the visa is issued.

---

## Who actually needs a blocked account

If you're a non-EU/EEA applicant for a German student visa or a residence permit for study purposes, you must show "secured livelihood" (gesicherter Lebensunterhalt) — currently defined as roughly €992/month, or about €11,904 for a year (2026 figure; the amount is tied to BAföG maximum rates and adjusts regularly — always verify on the official embassy or Auswärtiges Amt page for your intake).

You can satisfy that requirement in several ways — the blocked account is just the most common:

| Proof of funds | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Blocked account** | Deposit the yearly amount with an approved provider; it pays out monthly (typically in 12 instalments) | Applicants with personal/family savings |
| **Scholarship** | Official award letter showing the stipend meets or exceeds the threshold — a DAAD stipend of €992+/month qualifies | Scholarship holders — see our [DAAD guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/daad-scholarships-international-students-complete-guide) |
| **Sponsorship declaration (Verpflichtungserklärung)** | A person legally resident in Germany guarantees your support at the foreigners' office | Applicants with family/close friends in Germany (scrutinised more strictly) |
| **Bank guarantee** | A German bank guarantees the amount (rare, requires a German bank relationship) | Special cases |

Check with the German embassy or consulate in your country which options it accepts — practice varies slightly by post.

## How a blocked account works, step by step

1. **Choose a provider.** Commonly used approved providers include Expatrio, Fintiba, and (for some nationalities via partner banks) Coracle; some consulates also accept accounts at traditional German banks. Confirm your embassy's accepted list before opening anything — this step trips up many applicants.
2. **Open the account online.** You'll need your passport and basic personal data; identity verification happens by video or document upload. Approval usually takes a few working days.
3. **Transfer the full amount.** Roughly €11,904 for one year (2026 rate) plus provider fees. Note: several providers also bundle a "value package" with health insurance — you can often decline extras, but *incoming* students must have German health cover from day one, so a starter insurance package for the pre-enrolment weeks is commonly required.
4. **Receive the confirmation.** The provider issues a confirmation letter — this is what you submit with your visa application.
5. **After arrival: activate payouts.** You register your address (Anmeldung), open or link a regular German current account, then the blocked account releases a fixed monthly sum (typically 1/12 of the total). You cannot withdraw the whole sum at once.
6. **Top-ups and extensions.** If the rate increases or you extend your stay, the foreigners' office may require a top-up to the current threshold.

## Amount details and the common traps

- **The rate moves.** It was €11,208, then €11,904 for 2025/2026 intakes — and it will likely rise again. Always check the figure **for your application date**, not blog posts. If the rate rises between deposit and visa decision, you may be asked to top up.
- **For PhD researchers with a work contract, no blocked account is usually needed** — the employment contract itself proves your livelihood. This matters in Germany because most funded PhD positions are salaried jobs (see our [TV-L E13 salary explainer](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-salary-germany-tv-l-e13-explained)). The blocked account mostly concerns taught students and scholarship-free self-funded researchers.
- **Part-year deposits:** if your programme is shorter, some posts accept 11 months or a proportional amount — ask your consulate.
- **Fees:** expect roughly €100–€150 in setup/payout fees depending on the provider and package. Compare total costs, not just headline fees.
- **Currency risk:** you transfer in euros; your home bank's conversion rate and transfer fees add 1–3% on top.

## Blocked account vs German visa: how they fit together

The blocked account is one part of the student visa file, which typically also includes:

- University admission letter
- Completed application form and passport
- Proof of health insurance (start cover is fine before enrolment)
- Academic documents and possibly a language certificate
- Visa fee (~€75 — verify)

Processing times vary hugely by country — book your visa appointment as soon as you have admission; in busy posts the wait itself can take months.

## PhD applicants: when you can skip it

- **Employed PhD positions** (TV-L contracts at universities or institutes like Max Planck): the contract substitutes for the blocked account.
- **Full DAAD or equivalent scholarships:** the award letter suffices if it meets the monthly threshold.
- **Self-funded doctoral candidates without employment or scholarship:** yes, you'll need the blocked account (or an accepted alternative) at the study-residence-permit stage.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much money do I need in a German blocked account in 2026?**
The standard rate is around €11,904 for one year (about €992/month) at the 2026 level, plus provider fees. The amount is officially adjusted — verify the exact figure on your embassy's or the Auswärtiges Amt's page before transferring.

**Can I get my blocked account money back if my visa is rejected?**
Yes. Providers refund the deposit (minus fees) if you show the visa rejection. Read the provider's refund policy before opening the account so you know the exact procedure.

**Which blocked account provider is best?**
Expatrio, Fintiba and Coracle are the widely used speed-approved options, and traditional banks also work in some consulates. "Best" depends on your embassy's accepted list, fees and whether you need the bundled insurance — confirm the list with your consulate first.

**Do PhD students in Germany need a blocked account?**
Usually not if you hold an employment contract or a qualifying scholarship — those prove your livelihood directly. Self-funded doctoral researchers without a contract or scholarship generally do need one.

## Official source

Amounts, accepted providers and required documents change and vary by consulate. Always confirm on the official pages: the **German embassy/consulate in your country**, the **Auswärtiges Amt** visa portal, and your university's international office — not on blogs (including this one).
