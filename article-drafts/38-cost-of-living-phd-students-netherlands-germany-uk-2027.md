**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

A realistic monthly budget for a PhD student in 2026–27 is roughly £1,100–£1,600 in the UK (more in London), €950–€1,400 in Germany, and €1,300–€1,800 in the Netherlands — housing dominating every budget. Whether that's comfortable depends on your funding: stipends in all three countries are tight but workable outside the priciest cities.

## United Kingdom: stipends vs reality

A typical UK doctoral stipend sits around £19,000–£20,000 per year, tax-free — roughly £1,600/month, slightly higher with the London weighting. That covers a mid-tier city comfortably-ish and London only with flatsharing and discipline.

What moves UK budgets:

- **Visa-related costs before you arrive:** the visa fee and the Immigration Health Surcharge are significant one-off payments — our [UK student visa financial requirements guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/student-visa-uk-2027-financial-requirements-atas-graduate-route) lists the exact amounts you must show and pay.
- **Accommodation type.** University halls are convenient but rarely the cheapest for doctoral students; shared private flats usually win — compare options before signing anything.
- **One-off setup costs.** Deposit (usually 5 weeks' rent), guarantee requirements for international students without UK credit history, and winter energy bills.

Budget honestly for the first two months: setup costs routinely add £1,000–£1,500 before routine spending settles.

## Germany: still the cheapest of the three

Germany's combination of no tuition, subsidised student services and moderate rents keeps it the most affordable — with clear exceptions:

| City tier | Examples | Realistic monthly total |
|---|---|---|
| Affordable university cities | Leipzig, Dresden, Magdeburg, Kaiserslautern | €900–€1,200 |
| Mid-tier | Berlin (rising), Göttingen, Tübingen, Aachen, Darmstadt | €1,100–€1,400 |
| Expensive | Munich, Hamburg, Frankfurt, Stuttgart | €1,400–€1,900 |

Points that surprise newcomers:

- **Deutschlandticket** (currently around €49–€58/month, price subject to change) gives nationwide regional transport — a genuinely large saving in most cities.
- **Semester contribution** at universities (€100–€400/semester) often includes a regional transport ticket.
- **Mandatory health insurance** costs students roughly €120–€160/month; once your PhD is an employment contract, the employer shares the cost — a hidden advantage of E13 positions, as explained in our [TV-L E13 salary guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-salary-germany-tv-l-e13-explained).
- **Visa proof of funds:** if you need a blocked account for your German visa, the required amount (~€11,904/year for the current student rate — verify) is calibrated to roughly this budget. Details in our [blocked account guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/blocked-account-germany-student-visa-guide).

## Netherlands: highest costs, best salary floor

Dutch living costs run between the UK and Germany — but the housing market is the hardest in Europe for newcomers. The salary floor is the compensating factor: every PhD candidate earns the fixed employee scale (~€2,901→~€3,707 gross across four years), as set out in our [PhD in the Netherlands salary and contract guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-in-netherlands-employee-salary-contract).

Realistic monthly budgets:

| City tier | Examples | Realistic monthly total |
|---|---|---|
| More affordable student cities | Groningen, Nijmegen, Wageningen, Twente/Enschede, Tilburg | €1,200–€1,500 |
| Mid-tier | Delft, Eindhoven, Leiden, Rotterdam | €1,400–€1,800 |
| Tightest markets | Amsterdam, Utrecht | €1,700–€2,300 |

- **Basic health insurance is mandatory** (~€120–€140/month); many PhD employees qualify for the *zorgtoeslag* healthcare allowance, which offsets a chunk of it — check on toeslagen.nl.
- **Bikes are the transport budget:** €20–€80/month covers most candidates' transport needs.
- **Housing strategy:** university short-stay housing (often first-year only), then a competitive private market. Begin searching before you arrive; "huisjesmelker" scams target internationals — never pay deposits before verifying.

2. **Fix housing first**, everything else second. Rent is 40–60% of every budget above.
3. **Verify all figures at decision time.** Prices move; the ranges here are planning estimates for 2026–27.

## Hidden costs applicants forget to budget

Beyond the monthly totals, these one-off and recurring costs repeatedly catch new PhD students out:

| Cost | UK | Germany | Netherlands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visa application + health surcharge/ticket | Visa fee + Immigration Health Surcharge (often £1,000+ over a PhD's duration — verify) | Visa fee ~€75 | Reduced via university sponsorship; verify with HR |
| Proof-of-funds mechanics | Bank statements/maintenance loan evidence | Blocked account deposit (~€11,904/year) + provider fees | Usually none for salaried PhDs (contract is the proof) |
| Deposit + first rent | ~5 weeks' rent + first month | Usually 3 months' deposit (Kaution) + first month | 1–2 months' deposit + first month |
| Setup (bed, kitchenware, bike) | £300–£600 | €200–€400 | €200–€400 (plus a bike — budget €100–€300 second-hand) |
| Semester/admin fees | Usually included in tuition | €100–€400 per semester | None for employees |

Two practical mitigations:

- **Second-hand first.** German and Dutch student cities have active second-hand markets (local marketplace groups, university noticeboards) — most candidates furnish a flat for under €300.
- **Check student discounts even as a PhD employee.** Germany's Deutschlandticket and various cultural discounts apply broadly; Dutch universities extend sports and facility access to employees. Ask HR what your staff card covers — it's often more than you expect.

If funding is the bottleneck rather than costs, remember the funding model differs by country: salaried contracts in Germany and the Netherlands versus competitive studentships and scholarships in the UK — our [funding comparison](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-salary-stipend-comparison-by-country-2027) and the [fully funded scholarships listing](https://nextapply.co.uk/fully-funded-scholarships) are the practical starting points.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which country is cheapest for a PhD student: UK, Germany or the Netherlands?**
Germany is generally cheapest overall — no tuition, moderate rents and cheap transport. The UK is more expensive, especially London; the Netherlands sits in between, with the worst housing market of the three.

**Can I live on a PhD stipend in London?**
Yes, but only with shared accommodation and careful budgeting — a standard stipend roughly matches London's modest costs with little margin. A London-weighted stipend or additional funding makes it workable; check what your funder actually pays.

**How much should I save before starting a PhD abroad?**
Plan on a three-month buffer plus setup costs: roughly £3,000–£4,000 for the UK, €2,500–€4,000 for Germany, and €3,000–€4,500 for the Netherlands. Visa routes also require proof of funds — a German blocked account or UK financial maintenance evidence.

**Do PhD salaries in Germany and the Netherlands cover health insurance?**
Yes. In Germany, an employment contract splits the public insurance cost with your employer; in the Netherlands, you pay basic insurance yourself (~€120–€140/month) but may receive a government healthcare allowance depending on income.
