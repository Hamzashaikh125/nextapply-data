**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

The UK, Germany and the Netherlands fund PhDs in fundamentally different ways: the UK offers 3–4 year studentships on fixed stipends, Germany employs most doctoral researchers as salaried staff on TV-L E13 contracts, and the Netherlands treats every PhD as a four-year salaried employee. Which is best depends on your finances, field and plans after graduation.

---

## The one-line summary of each system

Before the details, the biggest structural difference — and the one applicants most often misunderstand:

- **UK:** You apply for a *project or studentship* (or self-fund). The university pays you a tax-free stipend, but you are almost never an employee. Standard duration is 3–4 years.
- **Germany:** The doctorate is often a *job*. You are hired as a research associate (wissenschaftlicher Mitarbeiter) on a collective-agreement contract (usually TV-L E13, frequently at 50–75% of full time) and write your thesis alongside the work. Duration is typically 3–5 years.
- **Netherlands:** Every PhD candidate at a university is a **four-year employee** with a fixed salary scale, pension contributions, paid holidays and usually a go/no-go assessment after the first year.

That difference shapes everything below — taxes, visa paperwork, what "funding" even means. For country-by-country stipend figures in one place, see our [PhD stipend and salary comparison](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-salary-stipend-comparison-by-country-2027).

## Duration and structure

**UK.** Three years is the classic figure, but four is now the funded norm for many studentships, and part-time doctorates stretch longer. You usually apply to a defined project or to a departmental scheme; a master's degree is commonly expected (or an integrated 1+3 route).

**Germany.** There is no single structure. Two paths dominate:

1. **Individual doctorate** — you find a professor who agrees to supervise you (most German PhDs). Contract, funding and duration are all negotiated individually.
2. **Structured programmes** — graduate schools and research training groups with coursework, cohorts and fixed funding.

Because the thesis is often written alongside a job, actual completion times run longer than the official minimum. Contracts are frequently extended in increments — a fact worth knowing before you plan.

**Netherlands.** Rigidly standardised: a 4-year employment contract, a first-year plan, a go/no-go review, and often a formal defence ceremony with the famous "laudatio." The predictability is a genuine advantage if you want certainty.

## Language and admissions

- **UK:** No language barrier academically. IELTS or equivalent is a formal visa/admission requirement for most international applicants.
- **Germany:** Research groups in STEM routinely work in English, but admin, contracts and healthcare are German-first. Some programmes require proof of German; many don't. Funding routes like DAAD often include language support — see our [DAAD scholarships guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/daad-scholarships-international-students-complete-guide).
- **Netherlands:** English is nearly universal in academia. Dutch helps for housing, healthcare and social life, but is rarely required for the PhD itself.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a PhD free in Germany and the Netherlands?**
Yes, effectively. German public universities charge no tuition for doctoral studies (only a small semester contribution), and in the Netherlands PhD candidates are employees, so there's no tuition at all. In the UK, tuition exists — funded studentships usually cover it, self-funded students pay it.

**Which country pays PhD students the most?**
Germany typically pays the highest gross monthly amount (E13 contracts), followed by the Netherlands' fixed scale, then the UK stipend. But UK stipends are tax-free, so compare net income and living costs rather than headline figures.

**Can I do a PhD in these countries without speaking the local language?**
In all three, yes for the research itself — especially in STEM. The UK needs no local language at all; in Germany and the Netherlands, English-taught programmes are common, but daily life and some administrative steps are easier with the local language.

**Which country is best for staying after the PhD?**
All three offer a post-study job-search period: around 18 months in Germany, 12 months in the Netherlands (orientation year), and the UK Graduate Route at roughly two years (longer for doctoral graduates — verify current rules, as immigration policy changes often).

## Official source

Fees, salary scales and immigration rules all change annually. Always confirm current figures on the official sites: **ukcisa.org.uk** and **gov.uk** (UK), **daad.de** and the relevant state's TV-L tables (Germany), and **werkenbij** pages of Dutch universities plus **ind.nl** (Netherlands).
