**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

Before applying to any PhD advert, check four things in this order: is the funding real and are you eligible for it (nationality limits are common), is the project start date and duration workable, who actually supervises and how often they publish, and what the advert doesn't say. Ten minutes of reading saves months of wasted effort.

---

## Why adverts need decoding, not just reading

PhD adverts are written by committees, inherited from templates, and optimised to attract applicants rather than to warn the ineligible ones off. As a result, the sentence that decides your fate — "funding available for home students only" or "applicants must already hold a master's degree" — is often buried in paragraph six.

Reading an advert properly means answering three questions in strict order:

1. **Can I get in?** (eligibility and funding)
2. **Is it good for me?** (project, supervision, conditions)
3. **What do they actually want?** (application requirements)

If you can't answer question one, don't spend a week on question three. Our guide on [verifying whether a PhD is really funded for international students](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-identify-funded-phd-accepts-international-students) goes deeper on the first step.

## Part 1: The funding paragraph — read every clause

This is where most international applicants go wrong. Adverts use precise funding language that hides big differences:

| Phrase in the advert | What it usually means | Your risk |
|---|---|---|
| "Fully funded studentship" | Stipend + fees covered, but check which fee rate (home vs overseas) | Medium — overseas fee gaps are sometimes dumped on the student |
| "Home-rate fees only" | International applicants must self-fund the fee difference (~£10k–20k/year in the UK) | High — the most common hidden trap |
| "Funding available on a competitive basis" | You compete for the same money after admission; no guaranteed place-with-funding | High — you may get in but unfunded |
| "Candidates should apply for external funding" | The university has no money for this project; you bring your own (e.g. DAAD, Commonwealth) | Very high unless you already hold a scholarship |
| "Bursary of £X per year" | Often partial — bursaries may cover stipend but not fees, or vice versa | Medium — always ask what exactly is included |

Practical rule: if the funding terms are ambiguous after two careful readings, email before applying. Our [supervisor email templates](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-email-phd-supervisor-uk-7-templates) include a funding-question version you can adapt in five minutes. Also cross-check whether an external scheme covers the project — schemes such as the [DAAD scholarships in Germany](https://nextapply.co.uk/scholarship-daad-germany) or the [Gates Cambridge scholarship](https://nextapply.co.uk/gates-cambridge-scholarship-2027-fully-funded) fund projects at participating universities directly.

## Part 2: Eligibility — the four quiet filters

- **Qualifications.** "A master's degree (or equivalent) in a relevant field" excludes direct bachelor's entrants in many UK and Dutch adverts. If you only hold a bachelor's, check our guide on [applying for a PhD without a master's](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/can-you-apply-phd-without-masters-degree) before skipping the advert — rules vary more than applicants assume.
- **Nationality and residency.** Some studentships are restricted by funder rules (e.g. certain UKRI and Commonwealth schemes). This is legal and common; it just isn't always in bold.
- **Degree class and GPA.** "2:1 or equivalent" is a hard filter at most UK universities. If your transcript sits below it, address the issue upfront in your cover letter: one honest line of context, then compensating evidence such as a strong master's or research project.
- **Publications.** "A strong publication record is desirable" means it isn't required; "track record of peer-reviewed publications" for an entry-level PhD advert usually signals a very competitive pool. Either way, most European PhDs expect no publications — see our [PhD without publications guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-in-europe-without-publications).

## Part 3: Project and supervision signals

Once you clear eligibility, judge whether the project is worth your three or four years:

- **Defined vs flexible.** "The successful candidate will investigate X, Y, Z" is a defined project — you inherit a plan. "The candidate will develop a project in the broad area of X" means you write the proposal yourself; expect an extra month of preparation and a stronger dependence on supervisor input.
- **Named supervisor vs "the supervisory team".** A named academic lets you read their last three years of papers; an unnamed team often means a rotating pool or a project designed around a grant that may still be in review.
- **Publication pattern.** Search the supervisor on Google Scholar: steady output with students as co-authors is a green flag; nothing since 2022 is a yellow flag worth one polite question at interview.
- **Start date and duration.** "October 2027, 3.5 years" is standard UK. Adverts saying "as soon as possible" often signal urgency — sometimes because a previous candidate withdrew, which is worth knowing before you resign from a job.
- **Location split.** "Based at [partner institute]" adverts may mean you live away from the main campus, or abroad — check before planning visas and housing.

## Part 4: Red flags in PhD adverts

- **Fees "to be confirmed"** with an application deadline two weeks away — you could win the place and then discover the funding gap.
- **No named funder.** Legitimate studentships name their source (UKRI, Wellcome, ERC, an industrial partner). "Fully funded" with no funder deserves a direct question.
- **Salary-style payment with no contract details.** In the Netherlands and Germany, PhDs are employees; if an advert mimics that language but offers a "stipend" instead, clarify your employment status, tax position and social insurance.
- **Excessive scope.** A three-year project promising fieldwork on three continents plus a methodological breakthrough is usually under-planned. Ambition is good; arithmetic matters.
- **Vague supervision.** No department named, no supervisor named, and a Gmail contact address — verify the department website lists both the project and the people before sending documents.

## The 10-point pre-application checklist

Run every advert through this table before you commit time:

| # | Check | Where in the advert | Green if… |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Funding source named | Funding section | Funder is explicit and the scheme is verifiable |
| 2 | Your fee status covered | Funding / eligibility | Overseas fees explicitly included (or you hold external funding) |
| 3 | Stipend amount and duration | Funding section | Amount ≥ national standard, full project length |
| 4 | Qualification requirements | Eligibility | Your degrees match, including any master's requirement |
| 5 | Nationality limits | Eligibility | None, or you qualify |
| 6 | Degree class / GPA floor | Eligibility | You meet or can evidence equivalence |
| 7 | Named supervisor | Project description | Verifiable academic with recent output |
| 8 | Application deadline & start date | How to apply | You have ≥ 3 weeks to produce proposal, references, transcripts |
| 9 | Documents required | How to apply | You can produce all of them (proposal? publications? language test?) |
| 10 | Contact for questions | How to apply | A university address, not a generic inbox only |

Items 8 and 9 decide your calendar. If references and a full research proposal are required, you need a month, not a weekend — our [12-month application timeline](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/masters-phd-application-timeline-12-months) and [research proposal structure guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-research-proposal-europe-structure-sample-pdf) will get both into shape in time.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I apply if the advert says funding is "competitive"?**
Only if you have a parallel funding plan. Getting admitted without money is a real outcome at many universities, and it can leave you holding an offer you can't afford.

**The advert doesn't mention international students at all. What does that mean?**
Nothing, in either direction. Absence of a restriction is not permission — ask, in one email, whether the studentship covers overseas fees before applying.

**Do I need to contact the supervisor if the advert lists everything?**
For defined, advertised projects, usually no — apply through the portal. Contacting is essential for flexible or self-proposed projects, and always welcome if you have a genuinely specific question.

**What if two adverts describe the same project?**
It's likely one project advertised on multiple boards. Apply once, through the university's own portal, and mention in your cover letter where you saw it.
