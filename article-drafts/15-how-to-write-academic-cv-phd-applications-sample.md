**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

An academic CV for a PhD application is a 2–3 page document that puts your research front and centre: education, research experience, publications, skills and references — in that logic. It is not a job CV. Cut the retail jobs and personality adjectives, and give every line to evidence that you can do research.

---

## What makes a PhD CV different from a job CV

Recruiters want reliability. Supervisors want research potential. That single difference should reshape the whole document:

| Job CV | Academic CV for PhD |
|---|---|
| 1 page, always | 2–3 pages is normal (2 is plenty pre-PhD) |
| Work experience first | Education and research first |
| Buzzwords: "team player", "go-getter" | Facts: projects, methods, tools, outputs |
| Designed to pass ATS filters | Read by a human academic — keep it plain |
| Interests section expected | Only include interests if genuinely relevant |

Admissions panels and supervisors typically spend 60–90 seconds on a first read. Your CV's only job in that window is to answer three questions: *Can this person handle the degree? Have they done anything research-like before? Do they fit my project?* Everything else is decoration.

## The correct structure (in order)

1. **Name and contact details.** University email if you have one, plus a professional personal address. No date of birth, photo or full home address for UK applications.
2. **Education.** Reverse chronological. Degree, institution, dates, classification or GPA, and — importantly — your dissertation title. A relevant dissertation is often the strongest research signal a pre-PhD applicant has. List notable modules or rankings only if impressive.
3. **Research experience.** Projects, dissertations, lab work, research assistantships. For each: dates, project title, one or two lines on what you did — methods, data, tools — and any outcome (report, grade, presentation, dataset).
4. **Publications and conference papers.** Full citations, your name bolded. Preprints, under-review manuscripts and posters all belong here, clearly labelled. If you have none, omit the section entirely — see our guide to [starting a PhD without publications](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-in-europe-without-publications); most European positions do not expect them.
5. **Awards, scholarships, funding.** Anything competitive, with the year and the selection basis if it was selective.
6. **Teaching, volunteering and relevant work.** Demonstrating, tutoring, or work that used your discipline. Bar work goes unless it paid for something relevant.
7. **Technical skills.** Programming languages, lab techniques, statistical software, languages. Be specific: "Python (pandas, scikit-learn)" beats "coding".
8. **References.** Two academics with name, title, institution and email. Ask them first — see our [recommendation letter request templates](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-request-letter-of-recommendation-email-templates).

That is roughly what a competitive applicant's CV looks like. Notice what it does *not* contain: objectives, photos, secondary school, soft-skill word clouds.

## Tailoring the CV to each application

For advertised PhD positions, spend 20 minutes per application reordering and rewording:

- Mirror the **methods and keywords** from the advert in your skills and project descriptions. Our guide to [reading a PhD job advert properly](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-read-phd-job-advertisement-before-applying) shows how to extract them.
- Move the research experience most relevant to the project to the top of its section.
- Adjust the dissertation line to emphasise the subfield the supervisor works in.
- For self-proposed projects, your CV should visibly support your proposal — the methods you list should match the methods your [research proposal](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-research-proposal-europe-structure-sample-pdf) promises.

## Mistakes that cost interviews

- **Padding with irrelevance.** Five years of hospitality jobs listed in detail signals you had nothing else to show, not diligence.
- **Vague project descriptions.** "Worked on machine learning" tells a supervisor nothing. "Built a CNN classifier reaching 84% F1 on a 10k-image dataset" does.
- **Missing dates or gaps left unexplained.** If you have a gap, handle it briefly and honestly — our [academic gap guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-explain-academic-gap-university-application) covers wording that works.
- **Design over substance.** Columns, icons and colours mostly waste space. Plain, single-column, generous white space wins.
- **Exceeding 3 pages.** If you have not yet done a PhD, you do not need 3 pages.

## Formatting rules that quietly matter

- One font (Calibri, Cambria or Times), 10.5–11pt body, single column.
- PDF only, named `Surname-Firstname-CV.pdf`.
- Consistent date format throughout ("Sep 2023 – Jun 2025").
- Bullet points for anything beyond one line; no paragraphs.
- Save the plain-text master somewhere you can update per application without starting over.

Pair the CV with a short, specific cover letter — our [PhD cover letter sample](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-write-cover-letter-phd-application-sample) shows the structure. The CV proves capability; the letter proves fit.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should an academic CV be for a PhD application?**
Two pages is ideal for most pre-PhD applicants; three is acceptable with a Master's, publications or substantial research work. One page is fine early on — never pad to fill space.

**Should I include my photo or date of birth?**
No for UK, Irish and US applications. In Germany and some other European countries a photo is still conventional but optional — omitting it will not harm you anywhere.

**Do I list my high school?**
No, unless you are applying straight from secondary school with essentially no university record yet. Otherwise, your degree makes it redundant.

**What if I have no research experience at all?**
Then your dissertation, final-year project and any data-heavy coursework carry the CV. Frame them rigorously: research question, method, dataset, result. That framing is what panels read as research experience.
