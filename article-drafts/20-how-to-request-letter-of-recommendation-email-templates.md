**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

Ask for a recommendation letter at least 4–6 weeks before the deadline, by email, with everything the referee needs attached: your CV, transcript, project/programme description, deadlines and submission method. Ask directly — "would you be willing to write a strong letter of support?" — so both of you know where you stand.

---

## The five rules of asking well

1. **Ask early.** Four weeks minimum, eight is better for professors juggling exam marking and grant deadlines. Late requests produce generic letters, or refusals.
2. **Ask in the right order.** Academic referees first for university applications; supervisors only when you lack academic options. Strong letters come from people who have seen you do the thing you are applying to do.
3. **Make it easy to say yes well.** Referees write better letters when you supply the raw material — our [academic CV guide for PhD applications](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-write-academic-cv-phd-applications-sample) shows what to include in the one-page reminder of your work with them.
4. **Ask the direct question.** "Would you be willing to write a *strong* letter?" gives a lukewarm referee a graceful exit. A reluctant "yes" can hurt more than a declined ask.
5. **Manage the logistics yourself.** Deadlines, links, submission systems, reminders — that is your job, never theirs.

## What to include in the request email

Your request should let the referee agree in one reply:

- **The ask**, stated in the first two lines
- **What you are applying to** (programme, university, degree) and why — one or two sentences
- **Deadline and submission method** (portal upload to their email, physical form, online reference system)
- **Attachments:** CV, transcript, personal statement or proposal draft, and any reminder of your work with them (graded essay, project report)
- **One line on why you're asking them specifically** — it helps them know what to emphasise

## Template 1 — current professor, course you did well in

> **Subject:** Request for a letter of recommendation — MSc application (deadline 15 January)
>
> Dear Professor Whitfield,
>
> I hope your term is going well. I am applying for the MSc in Data Science at the University of Edinburgh (deadline 15 January) and would be grateful if you would write a letter of recommendation in support of my application.
>
> I am approaching you because of my work in your Applied Machine Learning module (2024–25), where my coursework project on sensor-fault detection received the module's top grade, and because the MSc's focus on time-series methods builds directly on that project. I have attached my CV, transcript and a short summary of the project for your reference.
>
> The letter would be submitted through the university's online portal, which will email you a direct link; I would need it by 15 January. I completely understand if your schedule does not allow it — please let me know either way by the end of next week if possible.
>
> Thank you very much for considering this.
>
> Best regards,
> Priya Sharma
> BSc Computer Science, Class of 2026

## Template 2 — professor you haven't spoken to in a while

> **Subject:** Recommendation letter request — former student (MSc Public Health application)
>
> Dear Dr Osei,
>
> You may remember me from your Epidemiology module in 2023, where I completed a group project on malaria surveillance in northern Ghana (I have attached our final report to jog your memory). Since graduating, I have worked as a health-data officer at [organisation] for two years, and I am now applying for the MSc Public Health at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine.
>
> I am writing to ask whether you would be willing to write a letter of recommendation. Your reference would carry real weight because the course is epidemiology-heavy and your module is where that interest started for me. The deadline is 1 March, and submission is via an online form that takes roughly ten minutes — I would send you the link, my CV, transcript and draft personal statement immediately after your agreement.
>
> I understand it has been a while since we last spoke, so if you would prefer a short call or if you are unable to write a strong letter on this basis, I would be grateful for your honest answer.
>
> With thanks and kind regards,
> Daniel Mensah

Note what the third paragraph does: it acknowledges the time gap, offers to help, and invites an honest refusal. That paragraph is why the referee says yes.

## Template 3 — employer or internship supervisor

> **Subject:** Reference request — Master's application (deadline 20 February)
>
> Dear Ms Novak,
>
> I am applying for the MSc Environmental Engineering at TU Delft and would be glad if you would act as my professional referee. You saw my work directly during my eight-month internship, particularly the drainage rehabilitation project where I led the site data collection.
>
> The university asks for a reference covering my professional skills and readiness for graduate study; a short letter (one page) is entirely sufficient. The deadline is 20 February, and the letter can go directly to admissions at the email address below or through their online portal — whichever is easier for you.
>
> I have attached my CV and the course description so you can see what the programme covers. Thank you — your perspective on my practical work adds something my academic referees cannot.
>
> Kind regards,
> Amara Diallo

## After they say yes

- **Send a thank-you and a single consolidated email** with every link, deadline and document in one place. Referees juggling several students forget details; make yours impossible to lose.
- **Confirm submissions.** Most portals show reference status. If a deadline is four days away and nothing has arrived, send one polite reminder — "just flagging the 15 January deadline in case the portal email went to spam" — not a chase.
- **Report back.** Tell referees the outcome and thank them again. You will need them for the next application round, PhD supervisor contacts or job references, and referees remember students who closed the loop well.

## Choosing referees when your options are imperfect

- **No strong academic contact?** A Master's dissertation supervisor outranks a first-year lecturer; a seminar tutor who knows your name outranks a famous professor who doesn't.
- **Career-changer with old professors?** Template 2 works — lead with the work you've done since, and give them your CV early.
- **Mix academic and professional?** Most UK Master's applications accept one professional + one academic referee; check the programme page before assuming.

Poor references are among the [common reasons applications get rejected](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/why-scholarship-applications-get-rejected-12-reasons) — generic, lukewarm or late letters quietly sink otherwise good files.

## Frequently asked questions

**How far in advance should I ask for a recommendation letter?**
Four to six weeks minimum; eight to twelve for competitive scholarships with panel reviews. Ask once, early, rather than three times, late.

**Can I ask a professor who only taught me in one large lecture course?**
Only if you performed distinctively (top grades, memorable project) and remind them of it explicitly. Otherwise ask someone who knows your individual work — recognisable names on a reference matter less than specific evidence.

**What if a referee doesn't submit before the deadline?**
Send one polite reminder three to four days out. If it still doesn't arrive, contact the admissions office — many grant short extensions for references, but only if you ask before the deadline passes.

**Should referees see my personal statement or proposal?**
Yes, if you have drafts. A letter that echoes your own framing of your goals reads as coherent rather than as collusion — see our [SOP vs personal statement](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/sop-vs-personal-statement-vs-research-proposal) guide for what those documents should say.
