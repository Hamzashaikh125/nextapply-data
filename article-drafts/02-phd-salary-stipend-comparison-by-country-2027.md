## The number that matters: net income after real costs

Advertised figures mislead in three ways: some are gross salaries with tax and social security deducted, some are tax-free stipends, and none by themselves tell you what tuition, insurance and rent will consume. Before comparing offers, convert everything to this formula:

**Monthly net pay − rent − health insurance − tuition gap − commuting = real disposable income.**

A "lower" stipend in a cheap city can outperform a "higher" salary in an expensive one. Our guide to [building a PhD living-cost budget for the UK, Germany and the Netherlands](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/cost-of-living-phd-students-netherlands-germany-uk-2027) walks through the full calculation city by city.

## UK: the stipend model

UK PhD funding is a scholarship stipend, not a salary. It is **tax-free and National Insurance-free**, which makes a £19,000 stipend worth roughly the same disposable income as a ~£23,000 taxable salary. Two catches:

1. **Fee status.** The stipend usually sits *on top of* tuition, and international fee status can double or triple what the funder must cover. Always check whether a "fully funded" advert covers overseas fees — our guide on [spotting whether a PhD is truly funded for international students](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/how-to-identify-funded-phd-accepts-international-students) shows exactly what to look for.
2. **London weighting.** Some funders (including UKRI and Gates Cambridge) pay a higher London rate; others do not adjust at all.

## Germany: the employment model

Most German doctoral researchers in STEM are **employees**. The TV-L E13 pay group at 65–75% of full time means:

- Gross pay well above most stipends internationally, reduced by tax and social security
- Employer-funded pension and health insurance — you do not buy separate cover
- Paid vacation and strong worker protections

The same gross figure can look very different depending on your **contract percentage and pay step**. Read our [TV-L E13 explainer](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-salary-germany-tv-l-e13-explained) before comparing German offers.

## Netherlands: salaried doctoral employees

Dutch universities employ PhD candidates on a fixed four-year contract with a standard rising pay scale (CLB): roughly **€2,901 gross in year 1 rising to €3,707 in year 4**. After taxes and contributions, expect around €2,200–€2,600 net. Some positions still exist on grant-based scholarship terms with weaker rights — our [Netherlands PhD employment guide](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/phd-in-netherlands-employee-salary-contract) explains how to tell the difference in a vacancy text.

## When "small" stipends still win

- **MEXT Japan** covers tuition and airfare on top of the stipend, and several universities offer subsidised housing — the cash figure understates the package.
- **Swiss Excellence** scholars get CHF 2,450/month, but Swiss salaries and rents are proportionally high; treat it as comfortable only if your host city rent is manageable.
- **Fully funded scholarships** like [Gates Cambridge](https://nextapply.co.uk/gates-cambridge-scholarship-2027-fully-funded) add family allowances, airfare and development funding worth thousands per year beyond the headline stipend.

## How to compare two concrete offers

1. Write down both monthly **net** figures (ask HR or the funder for the net calculation — they answer this routinely).
2. Subtract rent for a realistic flat share near each campus (university housing office figures, not listings sites).
3. Subtract health insurance where it is not employer-provided.
4. Subtract any tuition not covered.
5. Divide what remains by local cost-of-living index. That final number — not the advert — is your quality of life.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a PhD stipend taxable?**
UK stipends are tax-free. German and Dutch contract salaries are taxable but include social benefits. US stipends are generally taxable income. The rule: salary = usually taxed, stipend = usually not, but verify locally.

**Which country pays PhD students the most in 2027?**
On gross pay, Switzerland and the Netherlands lead; on disposable income, Germany, the Netherlands and Scandinavia typically outperform the UK because stipends there are lower. Net-of-rent results flip by city.

**Can I survive on a UK PhD stipend in London?**
It is tight but common — London-weighted stipends (~£21,000) plus flat sharing make it workable. Non-London cities stretch the same money considerably further.
