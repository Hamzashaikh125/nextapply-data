**Direct answer box (first 50 words):**

A strong robotics portfolio is three to five projects that prove you can build, simulate or control a real robotic system end-to-end — each with code, a short video and an honest write-up of what failed. Supervisors shortlist on evidence of working systems, not on enthusiasm or course lists.

---

## What supervisors actually look at

Robotics faculty reviewing MSc or PhD applications spend minutes per candidate. In that window they check, roughly in order:

1. **Does anything you've built actually work?** A video of a quadruped recovering from a push, a manipulator inserting a connector, a locomotion policy surviving unseen terrain — ten seconds of working evidence beats a page of coursework descriptions.
2. **Can you code?** They will open the GitHub link. Clean repos with READMEs pass; empty or copy-pasted repos sink applications.
3. **Do you understand what you did?** The write-up matters less for polish than for evidence of judgement: what you tried, what failed, what you'd do differently.

This is why a portfolio is your most controllable application asset — unlike grades, you can build it deliberately over 6–12 months. Pair it with a tight CV that references each project in one line.

## The portfolio that works: 3–5 projects

| Project type | What it proves | Effort level |
|---|---|---|
| Simulation RL project (e.g. locomotion policy in Isaac Gym/MuJoCo, with a metrics table) | You can train, evaluate and honestly report learned controllers | 4–8 weeks |
| Classical control project (e.g. whole-body or joint-space controller on a sim humanoid/arm, with tracking-error plots) | You know the model-based foundations the field still runs on | 4–8 weeks |
| Hardware project (even small: a balance robot, a gripper with force feedback) | You can debug real systems — the scarcest skill | Variable, highest impact |
| Reproduction of a published paper (with deviations documented) | You read literature critically and can implement it | 2–4 weeks |
| Competition entry (e.g. simulation challenges, robotics competitions) | Teamwork under constraints; externally judged results | Seasonal |

Ideal balance for a humanoid robotics applicant: one learning-based project, one model-based project, one hardware or competition item, one rigorous paper reproduction. That spread answers the question every panel asks — *can this person do both worlds?* Our [humanoid robotics research topics list](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/humanoid-robotics-phd-research-topics) gives directions sized for exactly these projects.

## Presenting each project: the five-element format

Give every project the same five elements. Consistency signals professionalism:

1. **One-sentence claim.** "Trained a quadruped locomotion policy that walks on five unseen terrain types at 0.8 m/s."
2. **Video.** 30–60 seconds, no music, showing the system working (and, briefly, failing — honesty reads well).
3. **Repository.** Public, with a README covering setup, structure and how to reproduce the main result.
4. **Results table or plot.** Numbers against a baseline, however simple. "Better than a hand-tuned PD controller by X%" is a real result.
5. **Honest limitations paragraph.** 3–5 sentences on what doesn't work. This is the single most trust-building element — and the one most applicants skip.

## Common mistakes that sink otherwise good candidates

- **Course-project dumps.** Six repos of unmodified coursework with no READMEs. Curate: three strong items beat ten unexplained ones.
- **Missing media.** A robotics project without a video forces the reviewer to imagine it working. They won't.
- **Notebook-only ML work.** A Colab notebook fine-tuning an existing model is not a robotics project unless it closes a perception-action loop.
- **Overclaiming.** "Built a humanoid AI" for a simulated walking controller destroys credibility with exactly the readers who matter. Describe scale honestly.
- **Dead links.** Check every link the week you submit — including the one in your CV, cover letter and supervisor emails.

## A 6-month build plan

If you're starting from coursework only:

| Month | Goal |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Paper reproduction or structured course (e.g. a legged-robotics or RL specialisation) with all assignments made public and written up |
| 3–4 | One substantial simulation project with baselines, metrics and video |
| 5 | One hardware or competition item, however small — a balancing robot counts |
| 6 | Polish: READMEs, videos, a one-page portfolio site or pinned GitHub profile, links inserted into CV and applications |

Then connect it to applications: reference specific projects in your [humanoid robotics PhD proposal](https://nextapply.co.uk/articles/humanoid-robotics-phd-proposal-guide) and in every supervisor email — "project X is why I believe I can contribute to your Y project" converts a portfolio from decoration into argument.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many projects do I need for a robotics PhD application?**
Three to five well-presented projects are plenty. Quality and honesty of presentation matter far more than count — one excellent, documented project outperforms six unexplained repos.

**Is simulation-only experience enough if I have no hardware access?**
Yes, especially for humanoid and legged robotics, where much research is simulation-first. Add a small hardware item if you ever can (a cheap balancing or manipulator kit), but a rigorous simulation portfolio with real baselines is a legitimate path.

**Should I put my portfolio on a personal website or just GitHub?**
GitHub is where reviewers look; a simple one-page site linking the best three projects with videos helps non-roboticist committee members. Do both, keep them in sync, and make sure every link works before submitting.

**Do MSc applications need a portfolio too?**
A lighter version helps at selective programmes: one or two projects with code and video strengthens an application that grades alone can't. The same presentation format applies, just with fewer items.

## Official source

Application requirements differ by programme — some ask for portfolios explicitly, others only via the CV. Check each university's official admissions page for what may be submitted and how (links vs. attachments), and verify deadlines there.
